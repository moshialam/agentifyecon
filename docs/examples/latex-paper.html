Worked example — editing a LaTeX paper

Bibliography cleanup, table reformatting, and a careful section rewrite.

This walks through using Claude Code on a paper draft. The tasks are intentionally pedestrian — they’re the kind of thing you’d otherwise hand to an RA, except the loop here is minutes instead of days.

Note

The prompts below are verbatim — you can copy them. Replace file names with yours.

Setup

cd ~/Desktop/papers/wage-paper
claude

Before doing anything, give Claude an orientation pass:

“Read the project. Tell me what’s here, what paper.tex is about in one paragraph, and which auxiliary files (.bib, tables, figures) it depends on.”

This takes 30 seconds. The benefit is that subsequent prompts can refer to “the main paper” or “the auxiliary files” without you re-explaining each time.

Task 1 — bibliography cleanup

Bibtex files rot. Entries get added during a co-author binge and never cleaned up; some are cited, some aren’t, some have subtly wrong years.

“Look at every \cite, \citet, and \citep in paper.tex and appendix.tex. List entries in references.bib that are never cited. Don’t delete anything — just give me the list.”

You’ll get something like:

Unused entries (12 of 87):
  - smith2003
  - jones1999  (note: cited as `jones99` elsewhere — possible typo)
  - ...

The flag on jones1999 is a real win: it’s the kind of thing you’d miss on a manual pass. Now:

“Remove the unused entries from references.bib. For jones1999, fix the citation key inconsistency: replace \cite{jones99} with \cite{jones1999} everywhere, and keep the jones1999 entry.”

Read the diff before approving. Compile. Done.

Task 2 — table reformatting

You inherit a paper from a co-author and the tables are a mix of hand-written tabular environments, booktabs, and one orphaned longtable. You want everything on booktabs with a consistent style.

“Show me the source of each table environment in paper.tex and the standalone .tex files in tables/. Identify which use \toprule, \midrule, \bottomrule (booktabs) and which still use \hline. Don’t change anything yet.”

Once you see the inventory:

“Convert all \hline-based tables to booktabs style: \toprule at the top, \midrule between header and body and around any subgroup, and \bottomrule at the end. Keep column alignments and content untouched. Make sure \usepackage{booktabs} is in paper.tex.”

Warning

Here is where you must read the diffs carefully. Table edits are the classic place where an LLM “tidies up” content you didn’t ask it to tidy — a stray digit gets reformatted, a column header gets rephrased. Read every diff and reject anything that touches the content rather than the formatting.

Task 3 — a careful section rewrite

This is the one where you should slow down. Claude can rewrite prose beautifully and confidently, including arguments you don’t actually make. Treat this as collaborative editing, not delegation.

“Read section 3 (Empirical Strategy) of paper.tex. Don’t change anything yet. Tell me: (a) what the central identification claim is in your reading; (b) any place where the text references something that’s not in the data section or the appendix; (c) any sentences that are longer than 35 words or have more than two subordinate clauses.”

That’s an audit, not a rewrite. Read what it says.

If you agree with the audit:

“Rewrite only the sentences you flagged in (c) for length. Preserve every claim. Do not introduce new arguments. Do not change any numbers or citations. Show me the diff sentence by sentence.”

Approve, reject, or tweak each sentence individually. You stay the author.

Task 4 — compile and check

“Run latexmk -pdf paper.tex and tell me if there are any errors, warnings, or unresolved citations.”

Claude runs the build, parses the log, and tells you what’s broken. If a table now has a misaligned column, you’ll find out before you re-read the PDF.

What this saved you

  • Bibliography cleanup: ~2 hours of grunt work compressed to ~10 minutes of supervision.
  • Table reformatting: would have taken half a day; now ~15 minutes.
  • Section audit: the slowest task, but most valuable — Claude is willing to flag long sentences your eye no longer sees.

What it did not do

  • It did not decide what your contribution is.
  • It did not check whether your identification strategy is credible — it read what you wrote and described it back to you.
  • It did not fact-check your citations against the cited papers.

These remain your job.

Common traps in this workflow

Warning

Don’t let it “improve” prose you didn’t ask it to. Tell it explicitly what to change and what to leave alone. “Don’t introduce new arguments” is a sentence worth typing every single time.

Warning

Don’t trust new citations it adds. If it adds a \cite{...} for a paper you didn’t tell it about, treat that as a hallucinated citation until you’ve personally confirmed the entry exists in your .bib and the cited claim matches the source. See Hallucinations.

Tip

Use plan mode for multi-file edits. Hit Shift+Tab until you see “plan mode”, then ask. Claude will write a plan (“I’ll change these 4 files, here’s what each change does”), wait for your approval, and only then edit.
